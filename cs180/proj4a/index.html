Image Warping and Mosaicing

For this project, we are trying to do some warping and mosaicing. I shot three scenes by rotating my phone in place, and then recovered a homography for each pair of photos, and blended the photos into mosaics. I happened to be in Gateway when I needed photos, so I took three scenes: the lower level lobby (B1), a classroom, and a kitchen.

A.1 Shoot the Pictures

I took 3 photos of each scene with an iPhone at 1x zoom. To make sure the transform between views is a homography, I turned the phone about its own position and stayed in place. Neighboring photos overlap by roughly half, and I used the building photo from a previous project for the warp and rectification examples.

A.2 Recover Homographies

A homography maps a point (x, y) to (u, v) by computing (a, b, c) = H · (x, y, 1) and dividing: u = a/c and v = b/c. I set the bottom right entry to 1 so we can solve for the other eight. Each correspondence (x, y) → (u, v) gives two linear equations after multiplying out the division:

[x, y, 1, 0, 0, 0, −ux, −uy] · h = u
[0, 0, 0, x, y, 1, −vx, −vy] · h = v

Stacking these rows for n correspondences gives a 2nx8 system, which can be solved with least squares for h (Ah = b). Then I reshape h into the 3x3 matrix. As a sanity check, computeH recovers a known homography from five synthetic points to within 1e-6.

I clicked 9 to 13 pairs per image pair using the correspondence tool, choosing various sharp corners on different objects. The images on the right are all the center images for each scene.

A.3 Warp the Images

I warp by inverse mapping. The four corners of the input image go through H to predict the output bounding box. Then, every output pixel in that box is sent through the inverse of H back to the input image. Pixels that are outside the input become black and are marked as empty using a mask.

I then implemented two ways to sample the input at non-integer locations. Nearest neighbor rounds the coordinates and copies one pixel. Bilinear takes the four surrounding pixels and weights them by the fractional distances.

For the building warp above, nearest neighbor takes 0.08 s and bilinear 0.22 s. Bilinear reads and mixes four pixels per output pixel instead of copying one, so it is about three times slower, but it removes the jagged edges. I decided to use bilinear for the mosaics.

Rectification

To test the warp on one image, I clicked four corners of a rectangular shape and then computed the homography to send them to a different sized rectangle. For the building, the front of one of the balconies maps to 300x200; for the classroom, a square ceiling tile maps to 400x400. The output is then warped over a fixed region around the target rectangle. I did this because the floor corners of the classroom photo do not map anywhere sensible in a view that looks straight up at the ceiling.

A.4 Blend the Images into a Mosaic

To blend into a mosaic, the center photo stays the same and the left and right photos are warped into its frame. For blending, each warped image gets a weight equal to the distance from each pixel to the nearest edge of that image (the distance transform of its alpha mask). The mosaic is the weighted average of the 3 images.

The geometry lines up across all 3 scenes! Unfortunately the phone sometimes chose a different exposure for each shot, so some regions are brighter than others.
