Image Warping and Mosaicing / Feature Matching for Autostitching

This page has both parts of the project; in Part A, we stitch mosaics from hand-clicked correspondences, and in Part B, we find the correspondences automatically.

For this project, we are trying to do some warping and mosaicing. I shot three scenes by rotating my phone in place, and then recovered a homography for each pair of photos, and blended the photos into mosaics. I happened to be in Gateway when I needed photos, so I took three scenes: the lower level lobby (B1), a classroom, and a kitchen.

A.1 Shoot the Pictures

I took 3 photos of each scene with an iPhone at 1x zoom. To make sure the transform between views is a homography, I turned the phone about its own position and stayed in place. Neighboring photos overlap by roughly half, and I used the building photo from a previous project for the warp and rectification examples.

A.2 Recover Homographies

A homography maps a point (x, y) to (u, v) by computing (a, b, c) = H · (x, y, 1) and dividing: u = a/c and v = b/c. I set the bottom right entry to 1 so we can solve for the other eight. Each correspondence (x, y) → (u, v) gives two linear equations after multiplying out the division:

[x, y, 1, 0, 0, 0, −ux, −uy] · h = u
[0, 0, 0, x, y, 1, −vx, −vy] · h = v

Stacking these rows for n correspondences gives a 2nx8 system, which can be solved with least squares for h (Ah = b). Then I reshape h into the 3x3 matrix. As a sanity check, computeH recovers a known homography from five synthetic points to within 1e-6.

I clicked 9 to 13 pairs per image pair using the correspondence tool, choosing various sharp corners on different objects. The images on the right are all the center images for each scene.

A.3 Warp the Images

I warp by inverse mapping. The four corners of the input image go through H to predict the output bounding box. Then, every output pixel in that box is sent through the inverse of H back to the input image. Pixels that are outside the input become black and are marked as empty using a mask.

I then implemented two ways to sample the input at non-integer locations. Nearest neighbor rounds the coordinates and copies one pixel. Bilinear takes the four surrounding pixels and weights them by the fractional distances.

For the building warp above, nearest neighbor takes 0.08 s and bilinear 0.22 s. Bilinear reads and mixes four pixels per output pixel instead of copying one, so it is about three times slower, but it removes the jagged edges. I decided to use bilinear for the mosaics.

Rectification

To test the warp on one image, I clicked four corners of a rectangular shape and then computed the homography to send them to a different sized rectangle. For the building, the front of one of the balconies maps to 300x200; for the classroom, a square ceiling tile maps to 400x400. The output is then warped over a fixed region around the target rectangle. I did this because the floor corners of the classroom photo do not map anywhere sensible in a view that looks straight up at the ceiling.

A.4 Blend the Images into a Mosaic

To blend into a mosaic, the center photo stays the same and the left and right photos are warped into its frame. For blending, each warped image gets a weight equal to the distance from each pixel to the nearest edge of that image (the distance transform of its alpha mask). The mosaic is the weighted average of the 3 images.

The geometry lines up across all 3 scenes! Unfortunately the phone sometimes chose a different exposure for each shot, so some regions are brighter than others.

B.1 Harris Corners and ANMS

For Part B, the goal is to find the correspondences automatically instead of clicking them, following the paper "Multi-Image Matching using Multi-Scale Oriented Patches" by Brown et al. I used the Harris detector from harris.py on the grayscale image at a single scale. Since there is no strength threshold, we get about 30k corners.

To get fewer corners that are spread evenly over the image, I implemented adaptive non-maximal suppression (ANMS). For each corner, the radius is the distance to the nearest corner that is clearly stronger, meaning its strength is less than 0.9 times the other corner's strength. We keep the 1000 corners with the largest radius. Prior to running ANMS, we keep only the 5000 strongest corners for efficiency (since storing the distance matrix for all 30k corners would take ~8 GB).

B.2 Feature Descriptors

For each corner I take the 40x40 window around it and sample every 5th pixel to get an 8x8 descriptor. However, just before sampling, we need to blur the grayscale image with a Gaussian of sigma 2, so that each sample averages its neighborhood (so it doesn't pick one pixel). Then each descriptor is normalized to remove differences in brightness and contrast between the photos.

The examples below are 8 of the 1000 descriptors from each center image. I picked ones that later turned out to be good matches, so they are clearer than average. For each image, the 40x40 window is the top row, and the 8x8 normalized descriptor is the bottom row.

B.3 Feature Matching

To match a side image to the center image, we compute the squared distance between every pair of descriptors. For each descriptor in the side image, we find its nearest and second nearest neighbor in the center image. We keep a match only if first_dist / second_dist < a threshold (here I chose 0.6). This is because a correct match should be much closer than any other match, whereas a wrong match would be more undecided.

Most matches run in parallel lines, but there are still some wrong ones that cross others.

B.4 RANSAC and Automatic Mosaics

Since a few wrong matches would ruin a least squares fit, I used 4-point RANSAC. Each iteration picks 4 random matches, computes the homography from them (via computeH from Part A), and counts the inliers, which are matches where the side point mapped through H lands within 3 px of its corresponding point in the center image. After 5000 iterations I keep the largest set of inliers and refit H on all of them. I chose 3 px because Figure 3 from the paper shows that Harris corners are mostly off by 1 to 3 px. At first I tried 2 px, but it threw away many good matches.

The mosaics use the same warping and blending as Part A. For comparison, here are the manual mosaics from Part A on the left, and the automatic ones from Part B on the right.

The automatic kitchen lines up the back wall and the lamps better than my clicks did, but the chairs in the front are bad at the left seam. All of the inliers from RANSAC landed on the back wall, so the homography fits that plane the best. I did try raising the inlier threshold from 3 px to 6 px, and inliers then spread onto the counter and chairs. The chairs then came out clean, but the lamps doubled like in the manual mosaic. I held the phone by hand, so there is some parallax, so no single homography fits every depth.

The automatic version of the classroom is cleaner. The chairs and the door frame are doubled in the manual one, and the ceiling grid is slightly doubled in both.

The lobby looks the same both ways.

What I learned is that simple 8x8 patches and a ratio test already give mostly correct matches, and RANSAC throws out the rest. With a few hundred matches spread over the image, the automatic mosaics came out about as good as the ones from my manual clicks, and the automatic version of the classroom was even better!
